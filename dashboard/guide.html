Reading the Board

How to start it, what every element on screen means, what you can click, and what to do to make each part of the system show itself. ATRIA changes the order of attention — never the clinical care.

Starting it

Two commands. The first is a one-off.

make setup      # creates .venv and installs dependencies — once
make demo       # starts the server

Then open http://127.0.0.1:8000. Ctrl-C in the terminal stops it.

Give it about ten seconds

On startup the system trains its acuity scorer on 3,000 synthetic patients before the shift begins. Until that finishes the board reads "waiting for the first arrival…". That is not a hang. Patients start appearing right after.

What each part means

A-16
The ticket. The board reorders constantly, so this is how you follow a person rather than a position.
2 ↑ from 3
The priority band, 1 most urgent to 5 least, matching ESI. The arrow means a machine raised it and says from where. There is never a downward arrow.
the reason line
Why this band, in plain language — a fired rule, a moving vital, or the features driving the score.
RED FLAG
A deterministic rule fired on values someone actually recorded. No model output can suppress it.
MEASURE
A rule would fire if a missing vital were at its worst plausible value. Not an emergency — an instruction to go and take the reading.
HIGH MODERATE LOW
Confidence, from a conformal prediction set with a 95% coverage guarantee. No score is ever shown without one.
missing: …
Vitals never recorded. A score built on incomplete data says so on its face.
11m past safe wait
Held beyond the re-assessment interval for their band. It forces a human re-look; it does not by itself make anyone sicker.
IN TREATMENT
Taken through to a bay. Dimmed and sorted last, but still visible — a nurse needs to see who is where, not only who is queueing.
p95 14 ms
How long scoring is taking, 95th percentile. The budget is 400 ms.

Everything you can click

Four controls. Two live in the header, two on every row that has not yet been signed off.

ControlWhereWhat happens
? GUIDEheaderOpens the same explanation you are reading, condensed, inside the app. Escape closes it.
AUDITheaderToggles the audit trail panel. Every arrival, escalation, override, and discharge, each with its own hash and the hash of the entry before it, plus a live chain-integrity indicator.
ACCEPTon a rowSigns off the recommendation as it stands. The row's action buttons disappear and it is marked signed-off. Written to the audit log.
OVERRIDEon a rowOpens a modal to set a different band with a structured reason code. Choosing a higher number is a downgrade and shows a warning first.

Inside the override modal

Two dropdowns. The first is the new priority band, 1 to 5. The second is the reason, and it is a fixed list rather than free text — free text is not analysable later, and the whole point of the log is that someone can audit patterns in it.

Reason codeWhen you would pick it
reassessed_at_bedsideYou went and looked; the picture is different from the numbers.
clinically_wellVitals look alarming, the patient does not.
known_baselineThese readings are normal for this patient.
artefactA bad probe, a cuff on the wrong arm, a movement artefact.
resource_constraintTriage under genuine scarcity. Recorded honestly rather than disguised as a clinical judgement.
otherAnything else.
Why the downgrade warning exists

Lowering someone's priority is the one action no model in this system is permitted to take. Layer 0 can escalate, Layer 1 can escalate, Layer 2 can escalate — none of them can relent. Only a clinician can, and the interface makes that asymmetry visible at the moment it matters rather than burying it in documentation.

Five things to try, in order

Each one makes a different part of the system show itself. Together they cover every claim the pitch makes.

Watch a quiet patient climb

Pick any row at band 3 or 4 and note its ticket. Leave it for a minute or two of real time — the shift runs at 240×, so that is half an hour inside the department. Some patients drift the wrong way and reappear higher up the board carrying an arrow.

look for: ↑ from 3  ·  "SpO2 falling"  ·  "shock index"

Find a patient with missing vitals

Look for the orange MEASURE tag, or a row listing missing: fields. That patient did not get a reassuring score by default, and did not get flagged as critical either. They sit at band 2 with an instruction to go and take the reading.

this is "unknown is not normal", without flooding the board

Override someone, then read the log

Click OVERRIDE on any row, choose a higher band number so the warning appears, pick a reason, and confirm. Then open AUDIT and look at the newest entry: your identity, the reason code, the band before and after, and the hash chaining it to everything prior.

the chain indicator should still read intact

Kill the model

From another terminal, take the scorer offline. A banner appears across the top. The board does not blank and does not quietly serve stale scores — the deterministic gate keeps running, and every score drops to LOW confidence because the thing that produced confidence is gone.

curl -X POST localhost:8000/api/degraded/1
curl -X POST localhost:8000/api/degraded/0  # restore

Follow one patient all the way through

Pick a ticket and track it in the ticker: arrived, possibly escalated, then taken through when a bay frees, then left once treated. That is the full lifecycle, and it is why the queue drains instead of growing forever.

the ticker sits directly under the header, newest first
For a recording, use the scripted run instead

The live board is randomised, so no two runs are identical. make scenarios plays six fixed cases through the same engine — an ambiguous presentation, a paediatric threshold, a zero-history patient, a 3× surge, a clinician override, and a total model failure — and prints what happened. Same result every time, so a take can be re-recorded exactly.

What is happening while you watch

The board is replaying a simulated shift. Knowing the parameters makes the behaviour legible.

SettingValueWhy
Speed240×One hour of department time per fifteen seconds. A patient arrives roughly every ninety seconds.
Shift length3 hours40 arrivals, then a six-second pause and a fresh shift, so the board is never empty.
Treatment bays3When one frees, the highest-priority waiting patient is taken through.

Why the bays matter more than they look

An earlier version had no treatment model at all — nobody ever left the queue. Waits grew without bound, the queue-aging rule fired on everyone, and the entire board inflated to band 1. It looked like a broken triage system and was actually a broken simulation. Worth knowing, because it is the kind of thing that is invisible until you watch the screen for a minute.

Why the rows move

Four layers, each with a different amount of authority. The separation is the design.

Layer 0

Red-flag gate

Decides

Ten hard rules, each carrying a clinical citation — hypoxaemia, shock, consciousness, airway and so on. Pure logic, no model, no network, so it keeps working when everything else is down. Thresholds are age-banded: a systolic of 88 is shock in an adult and entirely normal in a three-year-old.

Layer 1

Acuity scorer

Recommends

Gradient-boosted trees over the twelve fields available within five minutes of arrival. Produces the starting band, the reason, and the confidence. It recommends only — it can raise a band and never has the last word.

Layer 2

Dynamic re-ranker

Re-orders

The reason this is a queue and not a label. It watches trajectory, not snapshots: how oxygen saturation, blood pressure and heart rate are moving across the last thirty and sixty minutes, the shock index and its trend, and how long someone has been held. Most of the movement you see comes from here.

Layer 3

Human authority

Decides

The nurse accepts, overrides or downgrades — and only the nurse may downgrade. Every decision appends to a hash-chained log: each entry embeds the hash of the one before it, so an edit or deletion anywhere breaks the chain and is detectable.

The invariant, in two lines

ATRIA may escalate on its own. It may never de-escalate on its own. That is not a policy in a document — it is a function every priority change passes through, with a test that fails if it is ever violated.

if source is HUMAN: return proposed   # nurse authority is absolute
return min(current, proposed)        # machines escalate, never relent

Driving it from a terminal

Everything the interface does is an endpoint, so the demo can be scripted rather than clicked.

GET  /api/audit?limit=60            the audit trail and its integrity
POST /api/degraded/1                kill the model · 0 restores it
POST /api/override/{ticket}/{band}?reason_code=…

A quick look at the queue without opening a browser:

curl -s localhost:8000/api/snapshot | python3 -m json.tool | head -40

If something looks wrong

What you seeWhat it means
"waiting for the first arrival…" and it staysThe scorer is still training. Give it ten seconds. If it persists past a minute, check the terminal for a traceback.
The board is empty mid-shiftA shift just ended. There is a six-second pause before the next one begins.
Nothing updatesThe websocket dropped. The page retries every 1.2 seconds on its own; a reload forces it.
Every row is at band 1Should no longer happen — it was the missing treatment model. If it reappears, the bays have stopped draining.
Port 8000 already in useAn older server is still running: pkill -f "uvicorn service.app", then start again.

Two things we found by looking

The model had learned that missing vitals are reassuring. Blanking heart rate, respiratory rate or systolic lowered the predicted risk — a silent undertriage path, in a system that promises the opposite. It was found by auditing rather than assuming, and is now clamped: an unrecorded vital can never score better than an average one.

One of our datasets encoded its own answer. In 143,000 real Iranian emergency records, every single most-urgent patient had zero recorded vitals, against 0.1% of the mid-urgency band — the sickest bypass the triage form entirely. A model given that data would have scored beautifully by learning hospital paperwork instead of physiology. It is excluded from training and kept as the cautionary example.

What it deliberately does not do

The boundary is what makes it deployable, and it is worth being explicit about.

  • No diagnosis. ATRIA never suggests what is wrong with a patient.
  • No treatment recommendations. It changes who is seen next, not what happens when they are.
  • No autonomous downgrade. Every reduction in urgency is a human decision, recorded with a reason.
  • No silent failure. Missing data, low confidence and degraded mode are shown on the face of the board rather than hidden behind a number.
On the patients you are watching

Everyone on the demo board is synthetic — generated from distributions fitted to 143,000 real Iranian emergency records and validated against real vital-sign sequences from Boston. The performance figures come from real patients; the people on screen do not, which keeps the demo reproducible and free of any data-licence question.
